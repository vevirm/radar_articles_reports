Twenty patterns that keep shaping research and innovation

  • Critical materials remain an enduring weak point for European industry

    What keeps happening: European laboratories repeatedly study ways to replace imported minerals in chips, electronics and vehicles. The fact that substitutes are still under investigation shows the dependency has not simply gone away.

    Why it persists: A new material must pass factory tests and reach reliable production before a concentrated supply chain can change.

    Why it matters, and when: This affects how quickly European research becomes manufacturing. 2026–2035: the scale of substitution is uncertain; assess real production and imports, not research claims alone.

    Counterforce: new recycling and materials research can gradually reduce exposure.

  • Europe repeatedly struggles to turn research into growing firms

    What keeps happening: Public banks and the European Innovation Council continue to support young technology companies. Earlier evidence describes the same gap between inventions and commercial scale.

    Why it persists: Founders need later-stage finance, trained employees, buyers and manufacturing partners. Any missing step can stop a good invention becoming a European company.

    Why it matters, and when: The pattern can persist through 2035 even while individual firms succeed. Measure company growth and production location, not only start-ups.

    Counterforce: patient public investment and large first customers can help bridge the gap.

  • European AI computing is built through a mixture of national and shared projects

    What keeps happening: National sites such as Greek and Nordic computing initiatives sit alongside European coordination and joint purchasing.

    Why it persists: Facilities can serve many countries, but each has different ownership, staffing, energy and access rules. Linking them is a long-term operational task.

    Why it matters, and when: Europe's research quality depends on usable shared access, not the number of national announcements. 2026–2035: integration may improve, but uneven access could remain.

    Counterforce: EuroHPC provides an existing basis for coordination.

  • Research security remains a permanent part of international science

    What keeps happening: National funders and universities increasingly require checks on sensitive research and overseas partnerships, while the EU promotes cooperation.

    Why it persists: Research groups must distinguish ordinary open work from knowledge that could be misused, and rules differ between countries.

    Why it matters, and when: The lasting challenge through 2035 is to protect genuinely sensitive work without blocking safe research. Outcomes depend on proportionate, understandable checks.

    Counterforce: common guidance can reduce contradictory national requirements.

    Outside-Europe comparison: a verified study examines changing research-security governance in Germany and the United States. We infer that European institutions will need consistent procedures; the article does not prove a specific EU-wide effect.

  • Several European technologies share exposure to decisions made in China

    What keeps happening: Evidence on Chinese strengths in robotics, electric vehicles and other industries points to an important shared outside competitor and supplier.

    Why it persists: Different European sectors may rely on overlapping materials, components or industrial tools. A single policy change could affect several at once, although their exposures differ.

    Why it matters, and when: Europe must examine exact dependencies industry by industry through 2035; the idea of one uniform Chinese advantage would be misleading.

    Counterforce: European specialisation, international partners and substitute suppliers can reduce some links.

    Outside-Europe comparison: a South Korean study examines mineral supply risks in its semiconductor industry. This supports the possibility of shared global exposure, not a measured loss for European companies.

    What keeps happening: European policy repeatedly returns to concerns about manufacturing capability and technological complexity.

    Counterforce: industrial partnerships and public finance can help firms cross the gap.

    Why it matters, and when: This is an ongoing operating requirement, not a one-off training problem. Through 2035, the skills gap may widen or narrow depending on sustained staffing.

    Counterforce: shared test facilities and public research institutes can provide support.

  • Green innovation must solve adoption, not just invention

    What keeps happening: European programmes repeatedly fund promising clean technologies, but use depends on buyers, infrastructure and total cost.

    Why it persists: When a new method requires a different grid connection, factory process or consumer habit, good laboratory results are only the first step.

    Why it matters, and when: Through 2035, trials involving real customers and operating conditions will reveal more than laboratory efficiency records.

    Counterforce: well-designed public trials and stable rules can reduce adoption barriers.

    Outside-Europe comparison: field data from Rwanda and Senegal show how actual purchase conditions matter in household energy uptake. The data were collected in 2015. We infer that European clean-technology trials must measure adoption; the findings cannot be transferred directly to European consumers.
